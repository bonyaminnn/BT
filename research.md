# Research — Bony Amin, Design Engineer

Research notes written before any design work. Everything below feeds the sitemap
(Phase 2) and the wireframe copy (Phase 3).

## 1. Benchmark scan — 5 premium portfolio/studio brands

How I read them: what they claim, what sits on the homepage, and how the page sells.

### 1. Tanuja Shastri — tanujashastri.com (the structural reference)
- **Positions as** a hybrid: developer by training, designer by obsession. The hybrid
  *is* the pitch — one person, both halves.
- **Homepage carries** a one-word greeting ("Hello") into a short identity paragraph,
  then a counted project list ("Projects (05)"), then testimonials. Nothing else.
- **Sells by** restraint and proof. No pricing, no service grid — the work and the
  client quotes do the arguing. Built in Framer; a serif display face against a clean
  sans, white and one accent. Copy rhythm is short declarative lines with lots of air.
- **Take for us:** the counted-work pattern and the air between sections. But a
  portfolio that wants *business* buyers needs the things she leaves out: services,
  process, price anchor, FAQ.

### 2. Anthony Fu — antfu.me (the design-engineer archetype)
- **Positions as** an open-source maker and design engineer. The whole identity fits
  in about four lines: where he works, what he builds with, which teams he's core to.
- **Homepage carries** identity lines, then signature projects, then writing.
- **Sells by** artifacts, not adjectives. The shipped things are the credential.
- **Take for us:** brevity above the fold. Four lines, not four paragraphs.

### 3. Tim Gesemann — design engineer at Adobe
- **Positions as** a practitioner inside a known product org — borrowed credibility.
- **Homepage carries** a minimal intro, a dominant project section, then a career
  timeline.
- **Sells by** trajectory: the timeline answers "is this person serious?" before any
  case study is opened.
- **Take for us:** we have no Adobe logo to borrow, so our credibility has to come
  from process transparency and outcome-led work cards instead.

### 4. Robin Noguier — interaction designer
- **Positions as** premium craft. The portfolio itself is the demo.
- **Homepage carries** immersive, highly art-directed case-study presentation.
- **Sells by** making the visit feel expensive. Reviewers call it the polish benchmark.
- **Take for us:** the site is the first deliverable. If our own page is slow or
  sloppy, no copy rescues it.

### 5. Premium studio packages (Clutch / agency tier — DD.NYC, Nixtio, PopArt and peers)
- **Position as** institutions: tiered packages, process diagrams, logo walls.
- **Homepage carries** a service lineup and visible pricing tiers. Premium entry points
  cluster at **$5k–$10k**, with tiered ladders running $10k/$20k/$30k at the top end
  and $7k/$13k/$19k mid-market. Premium retainer/subscription models start near
  $5k/month.
- **Sells by** de-risking: named process, named price band, named timeline.
- **Take for us:** this is the band we must sit against. Being two people is only an
  advantage if we show it as *less hand-off, faster cycles* — not as *less capacity*.

### Pattern across all five
Short identity line → proof of work → how it's done → one clear next step. The solo
portfolios skip services and price; the agencies skip personality. **The open lane is
the middle: a studio-grade offer delivered with a maker's hands.**

Sources consulted: Colorlib, Sitebuilder Report, Case Study Club, Lapa Ninja,
Fueler, Clutch, Magier, TL Design Studios, Michelle Jones Creative, Design Tennis,
Palmer Creative, Brand VM, Mag Cloud.

## 2. My audience

**Who they are:** founders, product leads and marketing owners at businesses that
already have a product or site (or are about to build one). They're commissioning a
build, not browsing for inspiration. They arrive from a referral, a search, or a
LinkedIn profile — and they're comparing us against an agency and a cheap freelancer
in the same afternoon.

**What they want**
- One team that can take a thing from blank page to live, without a translator between
  design and code.
- Speed they can plan around — a date, not a vibe.
- Work that looks like the tier they want to be perceived as.
- A named person who picks up the phone, not a ticket queue.
- To be told what they actually need, including when the answer is "less than you asked
  for".

**What they doubt** (the real buying friction is risk, not logic)
- *Capacity.* "Two people? What happens when you're busy, ill, or gone?"
- *Disappearance.* The loudest unspoken fear in this market: paid and then ghosted.
- *Depth.* "A designer who codes — is either half actually good, or both mediocre?"
- *Price opacity.* No number on the page reads as "it'll be whatever they can get."
- *Process.* No documented process is a standard red flag for buyers.
- *Handover.* Platform lock-in, proprietary CMS, being stuck with us forever.
- *Distance.* Remote and async — "will I be able to reach them?"

**What makes them buy**
- A portfolio piece that looks like their problem. The portfolio is where they ask
  "have you done something like what I need?"
- Named testimonials with real detail — not anonymous praise.
- A visible process with stage names and durations.
- A price band stated out loud, so they can self-qualify.
- Objection-handling placed *at* the decision point: response time next to the CTA,
  "you own everything" next to pricing, "no lock-in" next to the plans.
- Our own site being obviously well made. For this buyer the site *is* the case study.

## 3. Positioning

> **One sentence:** You pick us because you get a senior designer and a senior engineer
> as one two-person team — so your product is designed, built and shipped end to end,
> with no agency hand-offs, no translation loss, and no waiting in a queue.

Supporting frame: *studio-grade work, maker-grade speed.* Agencies have the polish and
the overhead. Freelancers have the speed and the gaps. We are deliberately the only
two seats in the room, and both of them are senior.

## 4. Three messages the homepage must land

**Message 1 — Designed and built by the same two hands.**
We do design *and* engineering, so nothing gets lost between a Figma file and
production. One team owns the outcome from first sketch to live site.
*Claimed in the hero, proven in the work cards.*

**Message 2 — Two people, end to end, faster.**
A deliberately small team means no account layer, no hand-off, no queue. Decisions
happen in the same conversation as the work, so weeks come off the timeline.
*Claimed in the statement section, proven in the process/feature blocks.*

**Message 3 — Senior work, with the risk taken out.**
Fixed scope, named stages, clear price bands, and you own every file and repo. The
questions this buyer is afraid to ask are answered on the page before they have to.
*Claimed in the plans section, proven in the FAQ.*

Under-5-seconds test: the hero must deliver Message 1 alone. Messages 2 and 3 land
within three scrolls.

## 5. The illustration world

**One place: the two-desk workshop.**

Every illustration in the brand is a scene from, or an object on, a single imagined
workspace — a quiet upstairs workshop with exactly two drafting desks pushed together.
Wide scenes show the room and the city rooftops through its tall windows at different
hours. Mid-size scenes show a corner of it: a desk with a screen mid-layout, a wall of
pinned wireframes, a shelf of labelled boxes, a plan chest. Spot illustrations are the
small objects that live there: a calliper, a pencil stub, a bulldog clip, a paper
aeroplane, a mug ring on a blueprint, a plant on the windowsill.

Nothing leaves that room. No abstract gradients, no generic tech iconography, no
dashboards floating in space — this is the only reason twenty-odd assets can be
generated as a coherent set.

**One consistent style.** Soft watercolour washes over fine, slightly imperfect ink
linework. Warm paper-white ground, visible paper grain, pigment pooling at the edges
of each wash, generous negative space, no hard outlines around the composition. A
restrained near-monochrome palette of warm greys and graphite with a single muted
accent used sparingly — one ink colour, one wash family. Soft natural daylight from a
consistent direction (window light, upper left). People appear only as two simplified
figures seen from behind or in silhouette, never with detailed faces, so the room
stays the subject.

**How it connects to the story.** The workshop *is* the positioning, drawn. Two desks,
not twenty — the room physically cannot hold an account team. Both halves of the craft
sit on the same surface: sketchbook next to keyboard, drawing next to build. The
hand-painted medium argues for care and human authorship in a category drowning in
generated gloss, and the single-room constraint shows the thing we sell most — focus.
A room progressing from morning light to lit lamps at night across the page also gives
us a quiet way to narrate process: discover, design, build, ship.
